Why the Japanese Yen Is at a 40-Year Low

The Japanese yen recently dropped to its weakest level in {{prn:40|forty}} years, trading at around {{prn:162|one hundred sixty-two}} yen to the US dollar for the first time since {{prn:1986|nineteen eighty-six}}.

The yen has been weakening for years, and is now at a point at which experts say the government should intervene.Japan's Minister of Finance, Satsuki Katayama, has also repeatedly said the government is ready to take action.

However, past government interventions to support the yen have only provided a short-term boost, and have not addressed underlying problems.

In April, for example, when the yen fell to {{prn:160.72|one hundred sixty point seven two}} against the dollar, the government sold about {{prn:$73 billion|seventy-three billion dollars}} in assets to support the yen.This brought the yen back up to about {{prn:155|one hundred fifty-five}} against the dollar — but failed to arrest the long-term decline.

So what are these underlying problems?

One recent problem has been the war in the Middle East, which has raised the price of oil.Because oil is traded in US dollars, Japan has had to pay more yen for each barrel, which weakens the currency.

But a more fundamental problem is that Japanese interest rates are very low compared to those of other major economies, and especially when compared against the rates of the US Federal Reserve.On June 16, the Bank of Japan (BOJ) raised its interest rate to 1% — the highest in 31 years, but still much lower than US rates, which have been around 3.5% to 3.75%.

The difference between these rates pushes capital toward the US and away from Japan, because investors borrow the cheap yen to make investments outside Japan that will bring better returns.

Although the BOJ is expected to raise interest rates again this year, US rates are also likely to rise, so the gap between the two will probably remain the same.

The weak yen makes imports generally more expensive, and contributes to Japan's cost of living crisis.But as long as Japan's interest rates remain low compared to other major economies, government interventions are unlikely to provide anything more than a temporary fix.
